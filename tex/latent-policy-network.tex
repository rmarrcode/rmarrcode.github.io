\begin{abstract}
Latent Policy Network is a reinforcement learning agent that generates its own
weights on the fly. It is tested in Melee Light, a browser recreation of
Super Smash Bros. Melee, against an opponent whose fighting style and
character are hidden and can change mid-game. The agent encodes what it has just
seen into a latent context, and that context produces the weights of the policy
it fights with so it adapts to a new opponent without further training.
\end{abstract}

\end{figure}

\section{Motivation}

A standard policy network carries one fixed set of weights, and so one fixed
answer to every opponent. High-level fighting game players, by contrast, must
constantly adapt to their opponents' strategies and respond accordingly.

If recent interaction reveals who the opponent is, the policy should be able to act
on that by producing different weights for the opponent in front of it.

\section{Related work}

The design is adapted from the Latent Program Network (LPN) of Bonnet and
Macfarlane, which learns a continuous latent space of programs: an encoder maps a
handful of input--output examples to a latent program, a decoder executes it on new
inputs, and at test time a gradient search through the latent space refines the
program until it explains the examples. I reimplemented the LPN optimizer
(\href{https://github.com/rmarrcode/latent-program-optimizer}{latent-program-optimizer})
before starting this project.

Latent Policy Network carries the idea over to reinforcement learning. Recent
interaction history plays the role of the examples, the latent $z$ plays the role of
the program, and the generated policy plays the role of the decoder. One difference:
inference here is amortized entirely by the encoder, with no test-time search,
so adapting to a new opponent costs a single forward pass.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Clément Bonnet and Matthew V. Macfarlane. ``Searching Latent Program
  Spaces.'' \textit{arXiv preprint}, 2024.
  \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08706}
\end{itemize}

\section{Architecture}

A context encoder reads the last 12 observations, and its output drives the actor.
Four ways of letting context shape the policy were compared:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \resumeItem{Static baseline}{A regular, non-adaptive actor-critic that
  ignores context.}

  \item \resumeItem{Hyper head}{The context generates the weights of the actor's
  final layer.}

  \item \resumeItem{FiLM}{The context modulates the actor's features with FiLM scale
  and shift parameters.}

  \item \resumeItem{Full hypernetwork}{The context generates every layer of the
  actor.}
\end{itemize}

The adaptive agents use a GRU context encoder. All agents are trained with PPO.

\begin{figure}
\includegraphics{imgs/latent-policy-network-system.svg}
\caption{The full system. The context encoder compresses the last 12 observations
into a latent $z$; a hypernetwork maps $z$ to the weights $\theta$ of the actor
that chooses each action, and the critic reads $z$ directly. Everything inside the
dashed box is trained end-to-end with PPO.}
\end{figure}

\section{The Melee Light environment}

Melee Light runs headless in a browser and is driven step by step from Python. The
learner controls player one; player two is driven by a scripted opponent that
presses the same 20 controller inputs.

\subsection{Rules}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \resumeItem{One exchange per game}{Both fighters spawn close together on
  the stage, facing each other, at 0\% damage.}

  \item \resumeItem{First hit wins}{The game ends the moment either fighter is hit.
  Landing the hit scores $+1$; being hit scores $-1$.}

  \item \resumeItem{No stalling}{If 240 frames (four seconds of game time) pass
  without a hit, the learner loses and scores $-1$. Waiting out the clock is never
  a safe strategy.}

  \item \resumeItem{Decisions}{The learner chooses an action every 4 frames, so at
  most 60 decisions per game, from 20 controller inputs: idle, four directions,
  jump, shield, jab, four tilts, four smash attacks, and four specials.}

  \item \resumeItem{Observations}{Each step the learner sees 30 numbers: for both
  fighters, position, velocity, knockback velocity, facing, whether they are
  grounded, damage, knockback, hitstun, hitlag, shield health and action timer,
  plus the distance between them. Nothing in the observation names the opponent's
  style or character.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hidden opponents}

Each game the opponent is drawn from eight scripted styles. After it has played at
least 8 steps, the style has a 5\% chance of switching on every step, so the
opponent can change in the middle of a game.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \resumeItem{Rushdown}{Closes distance, jumps in occasionally, and attacks
  up close.}
  \item \resumeItem{Approach and jab}{Walks in and uses jabs and tilts.}
  \item \resumeItem{Spacer}{Backs off when too close and pokes from mid-range.}
  \item \resumeItem{Zoner}{Retreats and attacks with specials from a distance.}
  \item \resumeItem{Counter-poke}{Shields at close range and pokes otherwise.}
  \item \resumeItem{Jumper}{Jump-heavy vertical pressure.}
  \item \resumeItem{Mirror}{Copies the learner's current input, flipped
  left-to-right.}
  \item \resumeItem{Anti-frequency}{Counters whichever kind of action the learner
  has used most.}
\end{itemize}

Characters are hidden too: each game the learner plays as Fox, Falco, or Captain
Falcon, and the opponent as Marth, Jigglypuff, Fox, Falco, or Captain Falcon. To
win consistently, the learner has to work out both the style and the matchup from
the last few moments of play.

\section{Results}

Every agent was trained on two seeds and evaluated over 32 games per checkpoint.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \resumeItem{Broad sweep, 12 updates}{All four architectures were trained
  briefly. The full hypernetwork had the best mean win rate, 15.6\%, but only just
  ahead of the static baseline at 14.1\%. Hyper head and FiLM fell behind the
  baseline.}

  \item \resumeItem{Follow-up, 24 updates}{The full hypernetwork and the static
  baseline were trained twice as long. The full hypernetwork won 29.7\% of games
  against 18.8\% for the baseline. On one seed the two tied; on the other the full
  hypernetwork won twice as often, 43.8\% against 21.9\%.}
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}
\includegraphics{imgs/latent-policy-network-winrate.svg}
\caption{Win rate in Melee Light against the hidden-style opponent pool, mean of
two seeds, 32 evaluation games per checkpoint.}
\end{figure}

Two seeds and 32 games per checkpoint is a small sample, so this is an edge, not a
settled result. It is still the only architecture with a repeatable edge over the
static baseline in this environment. An earlier version of the environment, which
used the game's built-in CPU as the opponent instead of hidden switching styles,
showed no reliable edge for any adaptive architecture. A fixed opponent leaves
nothing hidden to infer, which is exactly what generated weights are for.

\section{Next steps}

Despite the initial success of this method, I don't think my current formulation
fully harnesses the ideas behind the Latent Program Network. Several pieces are
still missing, and I'd like to expand on them.

\begin{table}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
   & Latent Program Network & Latent Policy Network \\
  \hline
  Encoder
    & A handful of input--output examples are compressed into a latent
      program $z$.
    & The last 12 observations are compressed into a latent context $z$. \\
  Decoder
    & $(z, x) \to y$. The latent program is executed on a new input grid.
    & $(z, o) \to a$. The latent context generates the actor weights that
      choose the action. \\
  Optimization
    & Gradient search through latent space at test time, refining $z$ until
      the decoder reproduces the given examples.
    & ??? \\
\end{tabular}
\caption{The two networks, stage by stage. In Latent Policy Network, inference is
amortized entirely by the encoder, so the test-time search that refines $z$ has no
counterpart yet.}
\end{table}

So far, Latent Policy Network draws on the encoder and the decoder, but it is
missing the crucial step: optimization. I have a few ideas for how that step
should work.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \resumeItem{Search in the moment}{At a given instant the agent considers
  several latent vectors, imagines how each one plays out, and acts on the one
  with the most favorable outcome.}

  \item \resumeItem{Reflect when time allows}{Pause between exchanges to
  reconsider the latent vectors seen so far and commit to the most suitable one
  going forward.}

  \item \resumeItem{Pin down the crux}{Work out what is actually gained by
  meta-learning weights instead of training to find a single policy.}

  \item \resumeItem{Self-play}{Replace the scripted opponent pool with self-play.}
\end{itemize}
