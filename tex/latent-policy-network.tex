\begin{abstract}
Latent Policy Network is a research framework for reinforcement learning policies
that generate their own weights on the fly. It targets rapidly changing 1v1
settings: the opponent has a hidden style that can switch mid-episode, and the
agent encodes its recent interaction history into a context that produces the
policy's weights, so it adapts to a new opponent without further training.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

A standard policy network carries one fixed set of weights, and so one fixed
answer to every opponent. When the opponent changes strategy partway through an
episode, the best a static policy can do is a compromise that is mediocre against
everyone.

The policy should learn to adapt to new strategies it hasn't seen before. 

\section{Previous Work}

The closest published relative is the \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08706}{Latent Program Network}, 
aimed at programming by example and ARC-AGI rather than at control. 
LPN factors inference into three parts: an encoder maps each input-output pair of a 
specification to a distribution over a compact latent space of implicit programs; a
decoder executes a latent program by predicting the output for a new input 
directly, with no domain-specific language anywhere in the
loop; and between the two sits a search over the latent itself. Pairs are encoded
independently and averaged, so the representation is permutation invariant to the
order of the specification and indifferent to how many pairs it contains.

\begin{figure}
\includegraphics{imgs/lpn-diagram.png}
\caption{Inference in the Latent Program Network. The encoder maps I/O pairs to a
latent program, the latent is refined by optimization until it explains those pairs,
and the decoder executes the refined latent on a new input. Figure from
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08706}{Macfarlane and Bonnet}.}
\end{figure}

The middle stage is what makes the architecture worth borrowing from. An encoder
trained to approximate a posterior over programs will guess wrong on a genuinely novel
task, so LPN treats its output as a starting point rather than an answer: because the
decoder is differentiable in $z$, the latent can be refined by gradient ascent on the
decoder's log-likelihood of the observed pairs. Test-time adaptation is therefore part
of the architecture instead of a fine-tuning procedure bolted on afterwards. The whole
system trains end-to-end with a leave-one-out reconstruction objective --- each output
is predicted from its input and the \emph{other} pairs, so the encoder cannot cheat by
compressing the answer --- plus a KL term against the prior. On programming-by-example
tasks it matches or beats in-context learning and test-time training, and on ARC-AGI it
doubles its out-of-distribution performance once search is switched on.

This project shares the skeleton and changes what the latent is for. Both systems
compress a handful of recent observations into a vector that determines behavior rather
than carrying one fixed set of weights, and both bet that the compression is where
generalization comes from. The differences are the interesting part. LPN's
specification is a static set of I/O pairs describing one program; here the context is a
running interaction history against an opponent whose hidden style can switch
mid-episode, so the latent has to be re-inferred continuously rather than solved for
once. LPN decodes its latent into an output, whereas the full hypernetwork decodes
into the actor's weights. And LPN can afford to spend test-time compute on gradient
ascent because a supervised I/O pair supplies a likelihood to ascend; a reward signal
does not, so here the encoder's single forward pass carries the entire burden of
adaptation. That makes LPN's own finding --- that poor initialization sends the latent
search into different local optima, which is why amortized inference from the encoder
matters --- a note of caution rather than a reassurance, since this setting is nothing
but the initialization.

\section{Architecture}

A context encoder reads the recent history of observations, and its output drives
the actor. The framework compares four ways of letting context shape the policy:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \resumeItem{Static baseline}{A regular, non-adaptive actor-critic
  (\texttt{static\_mlp}).}

  \item \resumeItem{Hyper head}{The context network generates the weights of the
  actor's final linear layer (\texttt{hyper\_head}).}

  \item \resumeItem{FiLM}{The context network modulates the actor's features with
  FiLM scale and shift parameters (\texttt{film}).}

  \item \resumeItem{Full hypernetwork}{The context network generates every layer of
  the actor (\texttt{full\_hyper}).}
\end{itemize}

Each is paired with a choice of context encoder: a mean over recent observations,
a flattened window, a GRU, or a small Transformer. Policies are trained with PPO.

\section{Environments}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \resumeItem{Repeated duel}{A vectorized 1v1 environment whose opponent has a
  hidden style that can switch mid-episode. Evaluation tracks reward immediately
  after a switch, during early adaptation, and once the policy has settled.}

  \item \resumeItem{Public suite}{Adapters for OpenSpiel matrix and turn-based
  games, PettingZoo rock-paper-scissors, MPE2, SlimeVolley, MAgent2 Battle, and
  Footsies.}

  \item \resumeItem{Melee Light}{Scripted opponents drawn from a pool of hidden
  styles --- rushdown, spacer, zoner, mirror, anti-frequency, and others --- and
  character matchups, switching mid-episode, so recent history identifies both
  style and matchup.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \resumeItem{Repeated duel}{In a two-seed sweep, a full hypernetwork with a
  GRU encoder reached a mean eval return of 42.3 against 33.3 for the static
  baseline. In longer same-budget runs evaluated over 256 episodes it reached a
  return of 66.9 and a 68.7\% win rate, against 58.8 and 64.7\% for the static
  baseline.}

  \item \resumeItem{Melee Light}{Against the scripted style pool, the full
  hypernetwork with a GRU encoder was the only architecture with a repeatable edge
  over the static baseline, winning 29.7\% of games against 18.8\%. Earlier runs
  against the built-in CPU showed no reliable edge for any adaptive architecture.}
\end{itemize}
